# Lesson Learning-Design Rules

Load this reference only after the lesson source has been audited and before deciding the exercise sequence.

## Evidence scope

These rules combine published learning research with Vocora's self-study product requirements.

The cited studies support individual principles such as retrieval practice, spacing, productive vocabulary learning, collocation learning, and relationships between lexical knowledge and listening performance. Correlational or predictive listening studies do not establish that a particular teaching sequence causes better comprehension. The cited studies do **not** experimentally validate one universal Vocora exercise sequence. The final sequence for a lesson is a design synthesis built from these principles plus the lesson's actual prerequisite graph.

Use the stable IDs below in the plan's `evidence_catalog` and in each exercise's `research_principle_ids`.

## Research principle catalog

### `retrieval_practice`

**Evidence:** Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention*. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255. DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x`.

**Supported claim:** retrieving previously studied material can improve delayed retention compared with repeated restudy.

**Design consequence:** after initial exposure, repeatedly require learners to retrieve target language rather than only reread it. Change the cue and response format when possible so the learner recalls the knowledge rather than memorizing a screen pattern.

### `distributed_practice`

**Evidence:** Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). *Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis*. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354-380. DOI: `10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354`.

**Supported claim:** spacing learning episodes generally improves later retention relative to massed repetition, with optimal spacing depending on the retention interval.

**Design consequence:** let Leitner own inter-session spacing. Inside one lesson, revisit high-value targets in different exercises rather than repeating the same drill back-to-back. Do not pretend one lesson session replaces spaced review.

### `multidimensional_vocabulary`

**Evidence:** Webb, S. (2009). *The Effects of Receptive and Productive Learning of Word Pairs on Vocabulary Knowledge*. RELC Journal, 40(3), 360-376. DOI: `10.1177/0033688209343854`.

**Supported claim:** receptive and productive learning affect different aspects of word knowledge, including orthography, meaning/form, syntax, grammatical function, and association.

**Design consequence:** do not treat a correct meaning multiple-choice answer as word mastery. Where the lesson supports it, include exact spelling, contextual selection, morphology, syntax/usage, associations/collocations, aural recognition, and production.

### `collocation_learning`

**Evidence:** Webb, S., & Kagimoto, E. (2009). *The Effects of Vocabulary Learning on Collocation and Meaning*. TESOL Quarterly, 43(1), 55-77. DOI: `10.1002/j.1545-7249.2009.tb00227.x`.

**Supported claim:** focused vocabulary tasks can produce gains in knowledge of both meaning and collocation.

**Design consequence:** when the source teaches natural word partners or formulaic chunks, create an explicit collocation/chunk objective and retrieve those combinations in more than one interaction form.

### `lexical_listening_support`

**Evidence:** Stæhr, L. S. (2009). *Vocabulary Knowledge and Advanced Listening Comprehension in English as a Foreign Language*. Studies in Second Language Acquisition. DOI: `10.1017/S0272263109990039`.

**Supported claim:** breadth and depth of vocabulary knowledge are strongly related to advanced L2 listening comprehension and predicted substantial listening-score variance in the reported study.

**Design consequence:** use lexical coverage to audit whether a listening task assumes language the learner has not encountered. This association does not by itself prove a causal teaching order; the source-specific prerequisite graph must justify any reordering.

### `spoken_word_recognition`

**Evidence:** Matthews, J., & Cheng, J. (2015). *Recognition of High Frequency Words from Speech as a Predictor of L2 Listening Comprehension*. System, 52, 1-13. DOI: `10.1016/j.system.2015.04.015`.

**Supported claim:** among 167 tertiary-level Chinese learners of English, recognition of high-frequency words from speech correlated strongly with listening comprehension; recognition of words from the third-thousand frequency level alone predicted 52% of the observed listening-score variance.

**Design consequence:** distinguish printed recognition from spoken-word recognition. An aural bridge is a Vocora design inference when the source task depends on recognizing learned forms in speech; this predictive study does not show that the intervention itself causes better comprehension.

### `pre_listening_support_limits`

**Evidence:** Chang, A. C.-S. (2007). *The Impact of Vocabulary Preparation on L2 Listening Comprehension, Confidence and Strategy Use*. System, 35(4), 534-550. DOI: `10.1016/j.system.2007.06.003`.

**Supported claim:** longer preparation was associated with higher vocabulary and listening scores, but statistically significant group differences were found only for vocabulary knowledge, not listening comprehension; vocabulary preparation also improved confidence.

**Design consequence:** vocabulary preparation is not a substitute for direct listening practice. Keep guided comprehension and full listening performance in the path when the lesson requires them.

### `productive_involvement`

**Evidence:** Hulstijn, J. H., & Laufer, B. (2001). *Some Empirical Evidence for the Involvement Load Hypothesis in Vocabulary Acquisition*. Language Learning, 51(3), 539-558. DOI: `10.1111/0023-8333.00164`.

**Supported claim:** in the reported tasks, greater task-induced involvement was associated with stronger target-word retention, with composition producing the highest retention among the compared tasks; time-on-task also differed and must not be ignored.

**Design consequence:** after adequate input and retrieval, use speaking or writing tasks that require learners to select and use target language. Do not claim that production is always superior under every matched condition.

## Lesson decomposition

Before sequencing exercises, identify the source-supported target inventory.

Typical target kinds include:

- `vocabulary`
- `multiword_expression`
- `meaning_contrast`
- `pronunciation`
- `morphology`
- `word_family`
- `collocation`
- `usage_frame`
- `grammar_warning`
- `reading_comprehension`
- `listening_comprehension`
- `aural_recognition`
- `speaking_function`
- `writing_function`
- `exam_mechanic`
- `transfer_task`

Use the source's own distinctions when they are more precise. Do not flatten a source warning, phrase, or task into a generic vocabulary item if the distinction matters for learner performance.

## Fixed opening exercises

### Exercise 1 — `vocabulary_intake`

Purpose:

- establish an initial form-to-meaning representation for every Leitner-eligible lesson item;
- expose pronunciation where available;
- activate unseen items into Leitner House 1.

Normal interaction direction is supported recognition. Use distractors that are plausible but do not depend on knowledge that has not been taught.

This exercise is an intake gate, not a mastery gate.

### Exercise 2 — `spelling_dictation`

Purpose:

- require exact form production for the same Leitner-eligible scope;
- connect sound or a constrained cue to orthographic form;
- expose fragile spellings and multiword boundaries early.

Prefer direct dictation for words and phrases. `correct-spelling` recognition may be used as scaffolding for genuinely difficult items, but should not replace typed production for the whole exercise.

## Content-dependent exercise priority

After the two fixed opening exercises, derive the smallest applicable sequence from the source. The order below is a priority heuristic, not a mandatory template.

### 1. Meaning in context

Use when the lesson expects learners to distinguish meanings, infer words from definitions, or select vocabulary inside sentences or passages.

Progress from recognition to cued recall when possible.

### 2. Morphology and word families

Use when prefixes, suffixes, parts of speech, derivations, or related forms are explicit source targets or prerequisites for later comprehension.

Do not create morphology work from etymological trivia that the lesson does not need.

### 3. Usage, grammar, register, and contrast

Use when the lesson teaches confusing forms, prepositions, transitivity, countability, register, syntax frames, or error warnings.

Prefer discrimination followed by correction or production.

### 4. Collocations and formulaic chunks

Use when later reading, listening, speaking, writing, or exam performance depends on multiword combinations.

Do not make the entire objective a single matching screen. A strong sequence may use teaching/contrast, matching, cloze, dictation, error correction, and freer recall across different items.

### 5. Reading integration

Use when a source passage consolidates earlier vocabulary or teaches a reading skill.

Teach prerequisite language before the passage when self-study comprehension would otherwise fail, but do not pre-answer the passage questions.

### 6. Aural recognition bridge

Use before integrated listening when the learner may know an item in print but still fail to segment or identify it in speech.

Suitable work includes phrase dictation, listen-and-identify, sound/word discrimination, and short local audio cues.

Use `spoken_word_recognition` for the distinction between printed and aural lexical knowledge. Use `pre_listening_support_limits` to avoid claiming that vocabulary preparation alone produces listening comprehension.

### 7. Guided listening

Use the source task structure when audio exists: speaker matching, table/form completion, gist/detail choice, short answer, truth classification, or other implemented patterns.

Early guided listening may include replay limits, focused prompts, or separated subskills. Reduce scaffolding before a later simulation.

### 8. Productive speaking and writing

Use after the relevant language has been introduced and retrieved. Require reuse of target vocabulary or chunks instead of introducing new target language.

Speaking and writing can be source-derived or a clearly labeled Vocora extension.

### 9. Exam mechanics

Use when the lesson contains an IELTS or other exam task whose success depends on non-language mechanics such as word limits, answer-type prediction, paraphrase recognition, form/table conventions, or forward information order.

Teach mechanics before the full simulation, not during it.

### 10. Full task simulation

Preserve the integrated task shape. Do not split a form, table, or coherent listening passage into unrelated micro-slides if doing so destroys the exam behavior being practiced.

Reduce hints and mid-task feedback.

### 11. Cumulative mastery

End with mixed retrieval when the lesson is intended to reach mastery.

The mastery check should:

- sample all important source targets;
- emphasize cued/free recall over repeated recognition;
- mix dimensions of word knowledge;
- reuse contexts or cues without copying prior screens verbatim;
- contain no new teaching target;
- record gaps for remediation or later Leitner review.

## Prerequisite rules

A later exercise should depend on an earlier one only when the earlier exercise builds a capability that the later task genuinely requires.

Strong prerequisite examples:

- vocabulary meaning -> contextual cloze;
- exact form -> phrase dictation;
- morphology -> reading that uses derived forms;
- usage frames -> error correction or production;
- collocations -> listening containing those chunks;
- printed lexical recognition -> aural recognition;
- aural recognition -> guided listening;
- guided listening + task mechanics -> full listening simulation;
- comprehension + retrieval -> speaking/writing production;
- all major lesson objectives -> cumulative mastery.

Weak prerequisite examples to reject:

- exercise A comes first only because it is shorter;
- every slide type must appear before mastery;
- a reading task depends on an unrelated morphology exercise;
- a listening task is delayed by vocabulary that never appears in it;
- a speaking task introduces vocabulary that no earlier objective prepared.

## Slide-mix rules

Choose slide types by cognitive action, not by visual variety.

Useful progression patterns include:

- meaning: `choice` -> `matching` -> `short-answer` -> contextual `cloze`;
- collocation: `teaching-card` -> `matching` -> `cloze` -> `dictation` -> `rewrite`;
- usage contrast: `teaching-card` -> `choice` -> `error-correction` -> `rewrite`;
- morphology: `teaching-card` -> `classification` -> `word-formation` -> contextual `cloze`;
- reading: text stimulus with `choice` / `truth` / `matching` -> `short-answer` -> `rewrite`;
- aural bridge: `pronunciation` / audio `choice` -> phrase `dictation`;
- listening: audio stimulus with `choice`, `matching`, `structured-completion`, `cloze`, or `short-answer` as the source task requires;
- production: `speaking-response` and/or `writing-response` after preparatory retrieval;
- mastery: mixed scored types with minimal teaching support.

Do not use every available slide type. `ordering`, for example, belongs only where chronology, process, severity, or another real order is a lesson target.

## Feedback and support

During learning exercises:

- provide immediate corrective feedback when it helps repair a misconception;
- explain why an answer is wrong when the distinction matters;
- keep the correct form visible long enough to encode it;
- allow replay for pronunciation/dictation when the objective is learning rather than simulation;
- reduce hints across later appearances of the same target.

During simulation or final mastery:

- avoid teaching inside the scored task;
- delay feedback until the coherent task is submitted when immediate feedback would leak later answers;
- preserve realistic answer constraints.

## Listening asset rules

If source audio exists, reference the verified source asset.

If source audio is missing:

- do not invent a URL or claim the original recording exists;
- record the asset gap;
- use an original parallel script/audio only when the caller or product policy permits a Vocora extension;
- label the extension clearly;
- do not call synthetic pronunciation or an original parallel script the source recording.

## Coverage and evidence rules

Every source target must have a stable ID.

Every exercise must include:

- `source_target_ids` for source-derived targets;
- `extension_ids` for Vocora-only additions;
- `evidence.source_refs` pointing to the lesson evidence used;
- `evidence.research_principle_ids` for applicable learning principles;
- `evidence.sequence_reason` explaining why the exercise sits at that point in the path.

The coverage audit must partition the lesson's source target IDs into covered and uncovered sets. Any uncovered target requires `validation.status: blocked` and an explicit blocker that records why the target cannot be covered; a warning or authorization alone is not sufficient.
